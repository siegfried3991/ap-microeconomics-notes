2. How to write an introduction

Use this flexible sequence: relevant opening → explain the debate → state your position with reasons. Three to five sentences often work, but that is a planning aid, not a known teacher requirement.

  1. Open with the actual issue. A contrast, observation, or brief situation works. Avoid “Since the beginning of time…” and unrelated quotations.
  2. Clarify the tension. What are the two plausible views? Define an ambiguous key term if needed.
  3. Give a thesis that answers the question. Include the reasoning your body paragraphs will develop. A concession can make the position more thoughtful.

Adaptable frame: “People often judge [issue] by [visible feature]. Yet [complication or competing view]. Although [reasonable concession], [your position] because [reason 1] and [reason 2].” Change the wording to suit the prompt; do not force every introduction into identical sentences.

Model introduction: everyday effort

A college acceptance or a public victory may appear to change a life in an instant. Such moments are memorable, but their visibility can hide the preparation that makes them possible. Although unexpected events can redirect a person’s future, everyday effort often has the greater influence because it creates opportunities and develops the ability to use them.

Why it works: sentence 1 establishes the topic; sentence 2 introduces the complication; sentence 3 concedes a real point, takes a position, and supplies two reasons.

Model introduction: pivotal moments

Habits usually give life its direction, but an unexpected experience can force someone to question that direction entirely. The debate is therefore not simply whether effort matters, but whether one event can change what that effort is directed toward. Although daily choices shape long-term progress, a pivotal moment can decisively redirect a life by challenging existing beliefs and prompting a new commitment.

A quotation is optional. If the assignment supplies one, explain the idea you are responding to. Do not spend most of the introduction copying it, and do not invent a quotation or attribution from memory.

3. How to write a body paragraph

Claim → context → specific evidence → analysis → link. If you use two examples, the pattern can become topic sentence → evidence → analysis → evidence → analysis → link (TEAEA plus a final link). Neither two examples nor a fixed sentence count is confirmed as mandatory here.

  1. Claim: argue one reason from the thesis. “Preparation allows people to turn opportunities into lasting achievements.”
  2. Context: supply only what the reader needs to understand the episode.
  3. Evidence: give the relevant action, decision, detail, or short accurate quotation.
  4. Analysis: explain how the detail produces the result and why that relationship supports your claim.
  5. Link: state what the example establishes about the prompt, with an appropriate limit.

Ask three questions after every example: What does this reveal? Why did that result follow? Why does it matter to my claim? As a practical starting point, give two or three analytical sentences after a concise example; adjust to the complexity of your point.

Model body paragraph

Everyday commitment can make a decisive moment more powerful by connecting individual action to an existing struggle. Before Rosa Parks refused to surrender her bus seat in 1955, she had served as secretary of the Montgomery NAACP since 1943 and investigated racial injustice. Her arrest then helped spark the Montgomery bus boycott. This background matters because it shows that the protest emerged from sustained engagement with inequality rather than a sudden discovery that discrimination was wrong. The boycott also required continued action by a wider community, so the significance of her refusal cannot be separated from the organizing that surrounded it. A pivotal moment can therefore accelerate change while still depending on years of preparation and collective effort. Earlier activism; boycott context.

Read the moves: sentence 1 makes the claim; sentences 2–3 supply evidence; sentences 4–5 explain its significance; sentence 6 links the example to a qualified argument. This is more persuasive than “Parks was brave. This proves effort is important.”

Summary versus analysis

Summary: “Walter rejects Lindner’s money.” Analysis: “By rejecting an offer that would improve the family’s finances, Walter treats dignity as something that cannot be exchanged for money. His decision reveals a change in his definition of success.” Analysis identifies a meaningful relationship instead of repeating what happened.

Counterargument frame: “Admittedly, [strong opposing point]. However, [limitation or distinction]. In [example], [specific evidence]. This suggests [why your position remains persuasive].” Answer the strongest reasonable alternative; avoid claiming that effort controls everything or that one event erases all previous experience.

4. Fifteen adaptable stories and examples

These are an evidence bank, not fifteen paragraphs to memorize word for word. Each entry distinguishes what happened from a possible interpretation. Historical examples support a reasoned argument; one case cannot prove a universal rule. Literary stories illustrate ideas rather than establish real-world statistics. Use hypotheticals only if the assignment permits them, and label them honestly. Class texts and well-documented history are usually stronger choices than a fable when both fit.

1. Rosa Parks: a moment rooted in years of action

History • courage, preparation, individual and collective change

The story: Parks joined the Montgomery NAACP and became its secretary in 1943. Her arrest after refusing to surrender her bus seat in 1955 helped spark the Montgomery bus boycott.

Points it can support: A visible turning point can grow from years of commitment. Alternatively, one principled action can become a catalyst when others organize around it.

Adaptable analysis: “Parks’s refusal shows how a single act can accelerate an existing movement rather than create its values from nothing.”

Keep it accurate: Do not say she was merely physically tired, discovered her self-worth on the bus, or acted alone to end segregation. Source / further reading.

2. Frederick Douglass: learning to read

Autobiography • education, persistence, freedom, awareness

The story: Sophia Auld began teaching Douglass the alphabet. After her husband stopped the lessons, Douglass continued learning, including through exchanges with local boys. Reading sharpened his understanding of slavery and made his condition more painful to recognize.

Points it can support: Education can expose injustice and expand a person’s sense of possibility, while greater knowledge can also create distress. His progress required repeated effort.

Adaptable analysis: “Literacy empowered Douglass by helping him understand the system restricting him, even before it changed his external circumstances.”

Keep it accurate: Do not compress his education and escape into one instant or suggest learning made him worthy of freedom. See Chapters VI–VII. Source / further reading.

3. Walter Younger: choosing dignity over money

Literature • identity, family, material success, integrity

The story: In Lorraine Hansberry’s A Raisin in the Sun, Walter loses money entrusted to a business partner. Later, he rejects Lindner’s offer to pay the family to abandon its move into a white neighborhood.

Points it can support: His final decision can be read as a shift from defining success financially to defending the family’s dignity. A crisis creates a moral test; his response gives it meaning.

Adaptable analysis: “Walter’s refusal reveals that maturity can involve changing what one considers valuable, rather than simply acquiring more.”

Keep it accurate: The house purchase predates the loss. Do not say the theft itself automatically improves him. Refer to Acts II–III. Source / further reading.

4. Atticus Finch: doing right without winning

Literature • moral courage, prejudice, limits of individual effort

The story: In Harper Lee’s To Kill a Mockingbird, Atticus defends Tom Robinson against a false accusation. Despite the defense, the jury convicts Tom.

Points it can support: An ethical action can be valuable even when it does not achieve its immediate goal. The verdict also shows how institutional prejudice can defeat a sound argument.

Adaptable analysis: “Atticus’s defense separates moral success from public victory: he acts on principle even though the verdict exposes the strength of racial prejudice.”

Keep it accurate: Do not claim Tom is acquitted. Use the novel’s trial chapters, especially Chapters 20–21, as the main textual reference. Source / further reading.

5. Malala Yousafzai: continuing to advocate

History • courage, education, resilience, youth leadership

The story: Malala advocated for girls’ education before a Taliban gunman attacked her in 2012. After recovering, she continued her advocacy and co-founded Malala Fund with her father in 2013.

Points it can support: Courage can mean maintaining a commitment despite danger. The attack increased international attention, but it did not create her original belief in education.

Adaptable analysis: “Malala’s continued advocacy shows that resilience involves preserving a purpose after adversity, rather than simply surviving it.”

Keep it accurate: Do not suggest suffering was necessary or beneficial in itself, or that one person solved educational inequality. Source / further reading.

6. Alexander Fleming: noticing an unexpected result

History of science • chance, curiosity, expertise

The story: In 1928, Fleming observed that mold contaminating a bacterial culture produced an area without bacterial growth. He investigated the effect and named the active substance penicillin.

Points it can support: Chance can present an observation, but expertise and curiosity help turn it into a discovery. Further development by other researchers was needed to make penicillin a practical treatment.

Adaptable analysis: “The unexpected mold mattered because Fleming treated an anomaly as a question worth investigating.”

Keep it accurate: Do not say he immediately produced a widely available medicine or did all subsequent work himself. Source / further reading.

7. Apollo 13: collaboration under pressure

History of engineering • teamwork, adaptability, resourcefulness

The story: After the 1970 mission’s oxygen-tank accident, the crew used the lunar module as a refuge. Engineers on Earth devised a way to adapt command-module carbon-dioxide canisters, and astronauts assembled the workaround using available materials.

Points it can support: A crisis can require revising the original goal and combining different people’s skills. Expertise becomes useful through clear communication and practical cooperation.

Adaptable analysis: “Apollo 13 demonstrates that adaptability is a disciplined response to constraints, supported by teamwork and technical knowledge.”

Keep it accurate: The mission did not land on the Moon; successful recovery was a changed objective. Source / further reading.

8. The Wright brothers: improving through experiments

History of engineering • persistence, feedback, innovation

The story: The Wright brothers tested wing designs in a wind tunnel in 1901, improving their aerodynamic understanding before their powered flights in 1903.

Points it can support: Persistence works best when it includes learning from evidence and changing methods. Repeating an unsuccessful design unchanged would not provide the same benefit.

Adaptable analysis: “Their progress shows that productive perseverance combines determination with a willingness to revise assumptions.”

Keep it accurate: Do not reduce their achievement to one inspired afternoon or claim persistence guarantees success. Source / further reading.

9. Nelson Mandela: endurance and negotiation

History • leadership, resilience, reconciliation

The story: Mandela spent 27 years in prison, was released in 1990, and became president of democratic South Africa in 1994 after participating in the transition from apartheid.

Points it can support: Leadership may require both commitment to a principle and negotiation about how to achieve change. A visible public breakthrough rests within a much longer collective struggle.

Adaptable analysis: “Mandela’s journey suggests that lasting change can demand endurance as well as the ability to negotiate a different future.”

Keep it accurate: Do not say he ended apartheid alone or that personal determination removed every political obstacle. Source / further reading.

10. Helen Keller and Anne Sullivan: a breakthrough in learning

Autobiography • education, mentorship, communication

The story: In 1887, Sullivan spelled water into Keller’s hand while water flowed over her other hand. Keller describes recognizing the connection between the signs and the thing they named.

Points it can support: A single realization can reorganize understanding, while teaching and continued learning make that realization useful. Support from another person can help unlock existing ability.

Adaptable analysis: “Keller’s realization illustrates how a breakthrough can emerge through the interaction of a learner’s effort and a teacher’s method.”

Keep it accurate: Do not call this a cure for deafblindness or imply she lacked thought, intelligence, or dignity beforehand. Source / further reading.

11. Wangari Maathai: small actions organized at scale

History • environment, community, empowerment

The story: Maathai founded the Green Belt Movement in Kenya in 1977. It linked tree planting with rural women’s needs and community participation.

Points it can support: A manageable local action can become more influential when people organize and sustain it together. Environmental problems can also be connected to livelihoods and social power.

Adaptable analysis: “Maathai’s work illustrates how ordinary acts gain wider significance when organized around shared community needs.”

Keep it accurate: Avoid unverified tree totals or suggesting tree planting alone solves every environmental problem. Source / further reading.

12. The Hare and the Tortoise

Fable • consistency, arrogance, talent and effort

The story: In Aesop’s fable, the faster hare becomes overconfident and sleeps during a race. The tortoise continues steadily and wins.

Points it can support: Ability can be wasted through complacency; dependable effort can overcome an advantage that is poorly used.

Adaptable analysis: “The hare loses not because speed is worthless, but because confidence replaces the effort needed to finish.”

Keep it accurate: This illustrates a moral; it is not scientific proof that slow people always outperform fast ones. Source / further reading.

13. The Shepherd Boy and the Wolf

Fable • honesty, trust, long-term consequences

The story: A boy repeatedly invents wolf attacks. When a wolf actually attacks, the villagers no longer respond to his warning.

Points it can support: Repeated dishonest actions damage credibility, so an earlier pattern can shape how others respond to a later crisis.

Adaptable analysis: “The boy’s final truthful warning fails because his earlier choices have destroyed the trust on which communication depends.”

Keep it accurate: Do not argue that ignoring a real emergency is morally justified; analyze the damage caused by dishonesty. Source / further reading.

14. The Lion and the Mouse

Fable • kindness, interdependence, underestimated abilities

The story: A lion spares a mouse. Later, the mouse frees the trapped lion by gnawing through the ropes of a net.

Points it can support: Power in one situation does not guarantee independence in another. Someone dismissed as insignificant may possess the exact ability a problem requires.

Adaptable analysis: “The mouse’s rescue challenges the assumption that social importance can be measured only by visible strength.”

Keep it accurate: Use it to illustrate interdependence, not to claim every kindness will be repaid. Source / further reading.

15. A group project that improves through honest feedback

Clearly invented hypothetical • feedback, leadership, cooperation

The story: Imagine a group whose first presentation rehearsal is confusing. One member asks classmates where they lose the argument; the group reorganizes the slides, divides responsibilities, and rehearses again.

Points it can support: The improvement follows a change in method prompted by specific feedback. Leadership includes listening and helping a team correct its weaknesses.

Adaptable analysis: “In this hypothetical case, criticism becomes useful because the group translates it into concrete revisions.”

Keep it accurate: Introduce it with “Imagine…” or “For example, a hypothetical group…” Never present it as your own experience or as research. Use a real example instead if the rubric requires it.

5. Choose and adapt evidence quickly

Preparation versus turning points: Parks, Douglass, Fleming, Keller. Courage and values: Atticus, Walter, Malala. Learning and innovation: Wright brothers, Fleming, Keller. Cooperation and social change: Apollo 13, Mandela, Maathai. Trust and character: the shepherd boy, the lion and mouse, Walter.

Choose evidence by its causal connection to your claim, not by how famous the person is. Learn five or six examples deeply before trying to memorize all fifteen. For each, recall one precise detail, two plausible themes, and one limitation.

6. Quick practice with answers

Try answering before revealing each response.

What three things should an introduction establish?

The issue, the debate or complication, and a defensible position with reasons.

What turns an example into argumentative evidence?

An explicit explanation of how the relevant detail supports your claim.

What is wrong with “Effort always guarantees success”?

It ignores external barriers and counterexamples. Try “Sustained effort often improves a person’s ability to use opportunities.”

Correct this claim: Atticus wins Tom’s case.

Tom is convicted. Use the defense to discuss courage despite defeat, or prejudice overriding evidence.

Explain Parks in support of preparation.

Her activism predates her arrest; connect that sustained commitment and wider organizing to the significance of the boycott.

Explain Parks in support of pivotal moments.

Her arrest helped catalyze a boycott. Acknowledge earlier activism while explaining why this event accelerated collective action.

Turn “Walter refuses money” into analysis.

His refusal prioritizes family dignity over financial gain, revealing a change in his definition of success.

Why is an unnamed university study weak evidence?

Readers cannot trace or evaluate it. Do not invent institutional authority; use a verifiable example.

Give a fair concession to an argument favoring effort.

Unexpected events can redirect a life, but preparation often shapes how effectively someone responds.

When can you use the group-project story?

When hypothetical illustrations are permitted and you explicitly label it as invented. It cannot substitute for documented evidence when that is required.

7. Before you hand it in

  • Does my thesis answer the actual question and give distinct reasons?
  • Does each topic sentence argue one of those reasons?
  • Have I used accurate, specific evidence rather than a name or vague reference?
  • Have I explained why the example supports my position?
  • Have I addressed a reasonable opposing view?
  • Have I avoided “always,” “everyone,” and invented facts?
  • Do my sentences say clearly what I mean?
